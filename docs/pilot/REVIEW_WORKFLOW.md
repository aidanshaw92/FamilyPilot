# Reviewer workflow: who decides what, and a session a real person can time

Status: 8 October 2026. Nothing here approves or publishes anything. Figures are generated by `scripts/pilot/review-workflow.cjs` (`review-workflow.json`) and held to the data by `review-workflow.test.ts`.

## The rules, in your words

1. **A person decides every fact that could be published.** There is no longer an "accepted without a person" tier. The 72 facts the gate used to accept on its own now go to a person: 23 of them still have somewhere to be published, and they are read as low-risk groups with a sample first.
2. **A model's reading is a flag, never evidence.** Two models agreeing that a claim is supported proves nothing, and the blind check found that 48 of 170 items that matched their quote verbatim still went beyond it. A flag moves an item *up* a tier. Nothing a model says moves an item down or out of the queue, and no model reading alone rejects anything.
3. **Only a mechanical failure rejects**, into a visible, reversible list: a number the quote lacks, a facility the quote does not mention, a page that is not the venue's. Today that is 0 items.
4. **The source stays in front of the reviewer.** Every card shows the venue's own words in the words around them, the page link, the date read, and the proposed meaning with every word the quote does not contain highlighted. A negation the quote lacks is highlighted too, and a negation the quote has that the proposal drops is called out. (The first version did not highlight "not", so a flipped yes was invisible; a planted error showed it.)

## Four kinds of decision

| Kind | Who | What it covers | Count (pilot) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expert** | A named expert; you only for the whole-venue closures and core-visit restrictions | Can refuse a date or a household, contradict a provider, put a price in front of a parent, or hide a venue by a "no" on access: whole-venue closures and core-visit restrictions (5), official hours (10), prices and entry (13), a "no" on access (5), one held item | **31** |
| **Trained reviewer** | A trained reviewer, not you | Any other high-impact item (a wrong "yes" costs a family a journey), any "no", anything an independent check doubted, anything a model flagged or that was narrowed because it went beyond its quote, and the rules that only inform (area closures, cautions, booking notes) | **50** |
| **Grouped, low risk** | A trained reviewer | A plain "yes" for toilets, baby changing, café, playground, Blue Badge bays and the like with a direct own-page quote, no check findings, no flag. Equivalent items are decided together after a sample is read (one in five, at least three); one rejected sample makes the whole group individual. A group of three or fewer is read item by item | **15** items: 2 groups of 4 (6 sample reads, 2 group approvals) and 7 read alone |
| **Rejected automatically** | Nobody | A mechanical check failed | **0** |

Removed from the queue because approving them publishes nothing: **51 parked** (no field can hold them yet), **6 merged** (the same sentence decided once), **18 deferred** (production already serves the same value from an active claim).

**Arithmetic:** 171 items − 51 − 6 − 18 = 96. Reads: 31 expert + 50 reviewer + 13 grouped (6 sampled, 7 alone) = **94 reads and 96 decisions** (the two group approvals). They sit on 38 pages.

**What this does to your time:** the expert tier is the only one that needs judgement beyond "does the venue's sentence say this, for this site, with these conditions?". Of its 31, the ones I would want you or a named delegate to decide are the **5 whole-venue closures and core-visit restrictions** (the Natural History Museum's closures, including 9 October; the Science Museum's 24–26 December closure and its pushchair rule; Discover's festive closure and its pushchair restriction); the rest are decisions an expert reviewer can own (hours against the provider's, prices, access "no"s). Two-person rule for anything that can take a venue out of a family's day: the reviewer decides, a second person confirms, and the audit trail shows both.

**What this does not do:** it does not make the pilot faster. It makes it *safer to hand on*. Grouping has very little to group at ten venues (2 groups of 4). Whether it saves anything at 50 is a hypothesis to measure, not a forecast.

## A session you can run and time

There is no measured review time yet. Nobody but me has used the review page, and I am not a trained reviewer. The kit below produces the first real figure. It is two packs, one per role, built on your machine from the stored pages (the packs carry venue page text, so they are never committed):

```
# 1. the full pack: every item, with its page context  (local only)
node scripts/pilot/review-triage.cjs --pack /local/full-pack.json --pages /local/pages.json,/local/gap.json --all

# 2. a pack per role, plus the manifest that stays with whoever runs the session
node scripts/pilot/review-workflow.cjs --pack-in /local/full-pack.json --role reviewer --out /local/pack-reviewer.json --manifest /local/manifest-reviewer.json
node scripts/pilot/review-workflow.cjs --pack-in /local/full-pack.json --role expert   --out /local/pack-expert.json   --manifest /local/manifest-expert.json

# 3. the review pages (one self-contained file each)
node scripts/pilot/review-pack.cjs --pack /local/pack-reviewer.json --out /local/review-reviewer.html
node scripts/pilot/review-pack.cjs --pack /local/pack-expert.json   --out /local/review-expert.html

# 4. after the session: export decisions in the page, then
node scripts/pilot/review-session-score.cjs decisions.jsonl --manifest /local/manifest-reviewer.json --audit audit.jsonl --json session.json
```

| Pack | Cards | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Reviewer** | 24 | 14 reviewer-tier items spread over as many venues as possible; 2 groups' sample items with the group approval; **4 hidden controls** |
| **Expert** | 12 | One of each kind of expert reason first, then the rest; the page shows the reason on the card |

Each is meant to fit a 30 to 45 minute sitting. That is a scheduling expectation, not a measurement.

**The hidden controls.** Four real items are copied with one realistic error that no mechanical check flags: two add a claim in ordinary words ("Suitable for babies from birth", "No booking is needed"), one turns a yes into a no, one changes a figure. They look like any other card (no finding chips, nothing in the text) and they are never written to the audit trail; the scorer reports only whether the reviewer caught them (rejected, edited or marked unknown) or approved them. Rationale: timing alone would reward a fast, careless reviewer. Speed and accuracy are measured together.

**What the scorer reports:** measured seconds per decision (median, 90th percentile, total) by tier; wall-clock minutes; what was approved, edited, rejected, marked unknown; how many decisions were undone; the control result. The audit trail it writes is the same tamper-evident chain as before.

**Proposed bar for a new reviewer** (a proposal, not yet a rule): catch at least 3 of 4 controls. Below that, every approval by that reviewer is second-read until a later session clears the bar. A reviewer who rejects everything is also failing, which is why the real cards are scored too (a high reject rate on cards the expert later approves counts against them).

**Proof that the kit works, and its limits.** I drove the real page with a scripted stand-in (not a person) in two modes. A careless stand-in that approves everything caught 0 of 4 controls. A careful one that rejects any card with highlighted words caught 4 of 4 once the negation highlight existed (3 of 4 before it). Both exported, scored, and left no control in the audit trail. That proves the pipeline. It says nothing about how long a human takes, or how many a human catches.

## Forecasting rule

Do not forecast 50 or 700 venues from the model figure (about 31 minutes to read all 99 held items) or from one session. The rule I propose: at least three sessions, at least two reviewers, a record of the tier mix in each, and the control result in each. Cost per decision is very different for a price than for a café. Until then the plan for 50 is "the same workflow, measured".

## Where each decision comes from, so nothing is lost

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Can a reviewer see the original page? | Yes. The words around the quote, the date read, and a link to open the page. |
| Can a decision be undone? | Yes. A later line withdraws an earlier one; a batch can be undone as a whole. |
| Can I tell who decided? | Yes. Every line carries the reviewer's name and the hash of the line before it. |
| Can a model approve? | No. |
| Can a model reject? | No. Only a mechanical check can, and a person can overturn it. |
| What happens to an item a model flagged? | It goes to a person at least at reviewer level, with the flag on the card. |
