# Reducing the review burden without weakening provenance

Status: 2026-10-08. **Superseded for planning by [`REVIEW_WORKFLOW.md`](REVIEW_WORKFLOW.md)**, which also puts a person on the 23 items that were previously accepted without one (the full human workload is 96 reads, not 74). Nothing here approves, publishes or writes anything. Figures are generated by `scripts/pilot/review-reduce.cjs` from the committed profiles, the triage routing, production's active claims for these venues and `semantic-verification.json`.

## The exact number

| Stage | Removes | Remaining |
|---|---:|---:|
| Held for a person (the gate, an independent check, or a verifier would not accept it alone) | | **99** |
| **Parked**: nothing in the app can hold it (no claim type, rule, hours or reviewed-data home) | 20 | 79 |
| **Merged**: the same sentence on the same page as an item already in the queue | 1 | 78 |
| **Deferred**: restates a value production already serves from an active claim | 4 | 74 |
| **Rejected**: a mechanical check failed (a model reading alone never rejects) | 0 | 74 |
| **Decisions a person must make** | | **74** |

Of the 74: **29 need an owner or expert** (13 prices and admission, 9 opening hours and closures, 4 accessibility, 2 practical rules, 1 pushchair rule: the ones that can refuse a date or a household, contradict a provider, or put a price in front of a parent), **39 are standard reviewer decisions** (a facility, access or activity statement where a wrong "yes" costs a wasted journey), and **6 are low-impact and clean** (decided as a group after a sample is read). They sit on **34 pages**. Two models read 72 of the 74 as supported and two disagreed; **that is a flag on the card for the reviewer, not evidence**, and it moves nothing out of the queue.

The earlier "99 items, 28 rules and 16 hours readings" were not 143 decisions. A rule or an hours reading is the structured interpretation of an item that is already among the 99; the reviewer confirms the dates, scope and "covers the core visit" on the same card. They are 28 of the 74 remaining.

**What the stages are allowed to do.** A stage may take an item out of the queue only if it cannot publish anything: parking, merging and deferring publish nothing, and a rejection is listed with its reason and can be overturned. **No stage moves an item from "a person must decide" to "published".** The 20 parked items are not lost: they stay in the profile as source notes and are reviewed the day a field exists for them.

I did not take the AI verification further, deliberately. It would be possible to clear another thirty or so items "because two models agreed". That is the shortcut this process exists to prevent: the same model family wrote and checked the proposals, the classes left (prices, accessibility, negatives, rules) are exactly the ones where a confident error costs a family a journey, and the current human-review requirement is the standard you set.

## What independent semantic verification actually found

Every proposal was shown to two independent models with only the claim and the venue's own words around it (no author reasoning, no status): Claude Opus, and Claude Sonnet (a third pass was attempted on a stronger model and failed on a usage limit). 186 items in two blind passes: all 99 held, all 71 accepted at the time, and 16 deliberately wrong claims planted among them.

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Planted wrong claims (polarity flips, an added day, a changed number, a dropped condition, "temporarily" made "permanently", a scope widened) | **16 of 16 caught by each verifier** |
| Accepted items with no person (71) that went beyond their own quotation | **21 (30%)**: 16 both verifiers agreed, 5 one verifier |
| Held items (99) that went beyond their quotation | **27**: 24 both verifiers, 3 one |
| Mechanical quotation matching alone | Passed every one of them |

That last line is the finding that matters. **A proposal that is verbatim-quoted can still claim more than the quotation says**, and it did in more than a quarter of the cases checked (48 of 170): "no facility to heat baby food or milk" attached to a baby-changing sentence; "ground floor, no RADAR key" attached to "there is an accessible toilet in the museum"; "the 4D Theatre is closed until 31 December 2026" when 31 December is the date printed on every event listing; "Open daily 10:00 to 17:50" with the same sentence's "Closed 9 October" dropped, which is tomorrow's closure; a café's picnic benches presented as the whole zoo's; "pregnant visitors are advised not to touch ewes" when the passage the quote was taken from stops at "Precautions for Pregnant Women Avoid". Two rules inherited the errors: the RAF Museum rule carried an `until` date that was a listing date, and Horniman's "Natural History Gallery closed for refurbishment" rested on a sentence that does not say it (the homepage does, so it became its own fact with its own quotation).

**What was done about it.** 46 proposals were narrowed to what their sentence supports (the original wording is kept on the fact as `amended.from`, so nothing is silently rewritten), one proposal was split into two facts, and 4 whose extra numbers came from surrounding text had that sentence added to their evidence instead. The 47 were then re-verified by both models: 42 supported on the first re-check, 5 needed a further wording change, and the second re-check supported 4 of those 5. Where a verifier still doubts an item (3 of 171), the item goes to a person with the doubt on the card, and an accepted item with any doubt is demoted to the queue (one was).

**Limits, stated plainly.**
- The verifiers share a model family with the author of the proposals, so some errors may be correlated. The planted claims are the only calibration, and 16 is small.
- They judge whether words support a claim. They cannot tell that a page is stale, wrong, or about a different site from the one in the catalogue. Those are source problems.
- Some "doubts" were artefacts of the passage shown (one reading of a quotation cut off mid-sentence). They were resolved by showing more of the page, not by overriding the verifier.
- The first pass cannot find what is **missing**. It reads claims, not pages.

## A practical process that does not depend on you reading every item

Three roles, each with a bounded job:

1. **Machine (done, repeatable).** Mechanical gate; independent quotation checks; blind semantic verification by two models; dedupe; parking; comparison with what production already serves. Output: a ranked queue and a card per item: the venue's words with their page context, the claim, the interpretation, both verifier verdicts, and why a person is needed.
2. **Trained reviewer (not you).** The 45 standard and low-impact decisions: approve, edit the wording to what the quote supports, reject, or mark unknown, from the card (time per decision not yet measured; see below). A reviewer needs no engineering knowledge, only the instruction "does the venue's own sentence say this, for this site, with these conditions?"
3. **Owner or expert (you, rarely).** The 29 high-impact items, and only those: closures and hours that can refuse a date, prices, accessibility negatives, and the one pushchair rule. Plus a **sample audit**: you re-read 1 in 10 of what the reviewer approved, always including every edit, and every item where the two verifiers disagreed. If the audit finds a reviewer approving something it should not have, the sample rises to everything for that reviewer until it does not.

Two further controls that need no effort from you:
- **Two-person rule for anything that can remove a venue from a family's day** (a whole-venue closure, a core-visit pushchair rule): the reviewer decides, a second reviewer or you confirms, and the record shows both. The Natural History Museum closure is the worked example in `HARD_EXCLUSIONS.md`.
- **Everything reversible and audited**: the hash-chained trail (`review-audit.cjs`) records every decision, edit and undo, and a decision can be withdrawn by a later line.

**What shrinks this at 50 and 700 venues, honestly.** Not the AI verification: it improves quality and the reviewer's speed, not the number of mandatory human decisions. What shrinks it is (a) **not collecting what nothing can hold** (20 of 99 here), which is a data-model decision; (b) **fewer high-impact items per venue** as the extractor handles the plain facility sentences production already publishes automatically (it missed 11 the profiles found); (c) **recurring classes** (the same step-free statement across venues) decided once as a rule, with the sample audit; and (d) **asking the venue** (the confirmation email already drafted) for the few facts a page cannot settle. Whether (c) gives the batching this set could not is a hypothesis to measure on the next set, not a forecast.

## Measured review time: still not measured

No one but me has used the review page, so there is still no measured time per decision and no basis for forecasting 50 or 700 venues. The model figure (about 31 minutes to read all 99, 34 minutes triaged) is words-to-read, not time. The protocol in `REVIEW_PROCESS.md` takes about half an hour of a real reviewer's time and produces the first real figure; I would do that with the 74-item queue, with the trained reviewer rather than with you.
